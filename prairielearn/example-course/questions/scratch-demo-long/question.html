Charging an RC circuit

A capacitor of capacitance C = {{params.c_uf}} µF is charged through a resistor of resistance R = {{params.r_k}} kΩ from an ideal DC source of EMF V₀ = {{params.v0}} V. At time $t = 0$ the switch closes and the (initially uncharged) capacitor begins to charge. Assume ideal wires, an ideal source, and that the resistor and capacitor are linear and time-invariant.

Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law around the single loop, the source EMF equals the sum of the drop across the resistor and the drop across the capacitor:

$$ V_0 = i(t)\,R + \frac{q(t)}{C}. $$

Since the current is the rate at which charge accumulates on the capacitor, $i(t) = \dfrac{dq}{dt}$, substitution gives a first-order linear ordinary differential equation for the charge $q(t)$:

$$ R\,\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = V_0, \qquad q(0) = 0. $$

Separating variables and integrating (or recognising the standard form) yields the well-known charging solution, in which the charge rises exponentially toward its final value $Q_\infty = C V_0$:

$$ q(t) = C V_0 \left( 1 - e^{-t/\tau} \right), \qquad \tau \equiv RC. $$

The quantity $\tau = RC$ is the time constant of the circuit. It has units of time: here $\tau = (R)(C) = ({{params.r_k}}\,\text{k}\Omega)({{params.c_uf}}\,\mu\text{F}) = {{params.tau_ms}}\,\text{ms}$, using the convenient fact that kilo-ohms multiplied by micro-farads gives milliseconds.

Differentiating $q(t)$ gives the current, which decays from its initial maximum $V_0/R$ as the capacitor voltage rises to oppose the source:

$$ i(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{V_0}{R}\, e^{-t/\tau}. $$

The voltage across the capacitor, $V_C(t) = q(t)/C$, therefore follows the same exponential approach to the source voltage:

$$ V_C(t) = V_0 \left( 1 - e^{-t/\tau} \right). $$

A few checkpoints worth sketching on the curve before you answer:

  1. At $t = 0$: $V_C = 0$ and the current is at its maximum, $i = V_0/R$.
  2. At $t = \tau$: the capacitor has reached $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of the source voltage.
  3. At $t = 5\tau$: the capacitor is within $1\%$ of fully charged — often taken as "steady state".
  4. As $t \to \infty$: $V_C \to V_0$ and $i \to 0$ (the capacitor behaves as an open circuit to DC).
